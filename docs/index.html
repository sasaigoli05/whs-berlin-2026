We did not take the same summit home

We attended overlapping sessions and left with different arguments. Rather than blend them into one voice, the reflections stay separate — including where they disagree about what the evidence supports.

What changed our minds
On evidenceWe arrived assuming the problem with traditional medicine was a shortage of evidence. Amie Steel reframed it as a question about who gets funded to produce evidence in the first place — and if nobody researches a practice, calling it unevidenced is a circular argument rather than a finding.
On scaleWe expected the biggest sessions to be about the biggest systems. Instead the pandemic financing panel ended on three small questions: are health workers paid, is anything reaching the last mile, are local teams in the room.
On technologyWe came in treating AI as a workforce solution. The workforce session turned it into a question about who captures the time it saves — the clinician, the patient, or the schedule.
Closing

What we carry forward

We did not resolve these in Berlin and we are not going to resolve them here. They are the questions that kept reappearing in sessions that had nothing else in common.

  • If $700 million is disbursed and $60 million is visible to the government receiving it, what exactly has been measured when a pledge is reported as met?
  • Who decides what gets researched — and can a practice be called unevidenced by a system that never funded the study?
  • When technology saves a clinician an hour, who gets that hour?
  • Is it better to launch an imperfect programme now, or wait for the missing resources while people keep getting sick?
  • What would have to change for a health argument to be heard by a Ministry of Finance rather than only by other health people?
  • Community-led monitoring was named as essential in four separate sessions. What is actually stopping it from being funded as infrastructure?
